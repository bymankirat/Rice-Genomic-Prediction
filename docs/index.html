Can you predict a rice variety's traits from its DNA?

Breeding is slow because a cross has to be grown out before anyone knows whether it was worth making. Genomic prediction tries to shorten that: learn the link between markers and traits in varieties you have already measured, then score new ones from DNA alone. This tests how well it works on 413 rice varieties from 82 countries, and where it stops working, which is the part that decides whether a breeding programme can use it.

At a glance

The panel, and what survived quality control

The Rice Diversity Panel 1 (Zhao et al. 2011) is the standard public set for this question: diverse varieties, one genotyping array, 34 traits measured in a common garden. Markers that are rarely called or almost never vary carry no information and are removed before anything else.

Rice is not one population

Cultivated rice splits into groups that separated long ago: indica, aus, the temperate and tropical japonicas, and the aromatics. They differ in markers and in traits at the same time, which is exactly the situation that produces false associations, and the reason the prediction test further down is split in two.

Does the association analysis work? Ask genes we already know

Before trusting any new association, the method has to recover old ones. Three rice genes are textbook: sd1, the semi-dwarfing gene behind the Green Revolution; Waxy, which sets amylose content and so how sticky the rice cooks; and GS3, the main grain-length gene. If a genome scan for those traits does not light up at those positions, nothing else it reports is worth reading.

How accurately can traits be predicted?

Accuracy is the correlation between predicted and observed values in varieties the model never saw, which is what breeders use. Two baselines run in the same folds: predicting the training average, and using only 100 markers. Without them, an impressive-looking correlation says nothing about whether the markers earned it.

The hard test: a rice group the model has never seen

Random cross-validation mixes relatives across training and test sets, so part of the accuracy is family resemblance rather than biology. The honest test for a breeding programme working on new material is to hold out an entire genetic group. The gap between the two is the part of the usual headline number that does not transfer.

What this cannot say

  • One environment. These traits were measured in particular field trials. A model trained there does not account for genotype-by-environment interaction, which is often as large as the genetic effect itself.
  • Markers are not causes. An associated marker is usually just near whatever matters. Positions here point to regions, not to genes, and the known-gene checks are deliberately given a one-megabase window.
  • 44,000 markers is sparse for a genome of 400 million bases. Modern breeding uses sequencing, which captures rarer variants that an old array misses.
  • Accuracy is bounded by heritability. A trait measured with a lot of error cannot be predicted well by any model, so a low number can be the phenotype's fault rather than the method's.
